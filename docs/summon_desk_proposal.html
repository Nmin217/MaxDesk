1Summary

The project is a Humanscale electric sit-stand desk on a four-module swerve base. You tap your location on a phone, and the desk drives to you around people and furniture, parks with its keyboard side toward you and its monitor's back forward, and later drives back to a dock and charges itself. It stays at your height the whole time. It does not lower itself first, so nothing has to be wired into the desk's controls.

The software side is far along: a browser simulator that has been stress-tested for collisions, tight gaps, people in the way, parking, return trips, charging, and now a map that it remembers between trips. One caveat on the code: the robot-side software should be Java on WPILib. I have ported the planner, safety supervisor and map memory into a tested Java core (it reproduces the simulator's routes). The path follower, the state machine and the WPILib wiring still need porting, and the earlier Python package is out of date. What remains is almost entirely physical: the drive base, the safety chain, the UPS and dock, and keeping a desk at standing height stable while it moves.

2Goal and success criteria

Goal. On request, move the desk to a person safely and unattended, park it in a useful pose, and later return and recharge without help. The desk's height is the user's business: the robot never changes it.

The project is done when, on real hardware, all of these hold:

  1. No contact. Over a fixed test course with people walking and standing, nothing and no one is touched. The hardwired E-stop stops all motion in under a second, with no software involved.
  2. Gets there. From the dock to a target anywhere in the test room, including a 70 cm gap, with no human help in at least 9 of 10 trials. After the room has been mapped, the first planned route does not cross a known obstacle.
  3. Parks usefully. Stops facing you with the table horizontal where possible and the monitor's back forward, within a few centimeters of the planned spot.
  4. Stays stable at standing height. No tipping, wobble or sliding items at the speed and braking limits, with the desk left at its tallest working height and a realistic monitor load.
  5. Docks and charges. Both batteries charge by themselves, and it goes home on its own when either is low.

3System overview

This follows your wiring diagram, updated for two changes: a UPS replaces the Anker power station, and the desk stays manual, so there is no desk control board or handset wiring. The robot has a 12 V FRC-style drivetrain, a lidar and onboard-computer domain on UPS power, and a hardwired E-stop that sits above both.

Code and language

The robot-side code should be Java on WPILib, which is the best-supported path for an FRC-style robot: swerve templates, simulation, logging and REVLib all treat Java as first class. A sensible split:

  • roboRIO (Java): swerve drive from the REV MAXSwerve template, the safety supervisor, path following, the state machine and the charging interlock.
  • Coprocessor (Pi 5 or Jetson): the lidar drivers (vendor SDKs are usually C++ or Python) and UPS monitoring through Network UPS Tools, publishing scans and power state over NetworkTables.
  • Planner: light enough to run in Java on either side. It takes about 2 ms on a laptop JVM; the roboRIO is much slower, so measure it there before deciding.

The Java core (summon_java.zip) contains the planner, supervisor and map memory with 16 passing checks, and a skeleton of the WPILib subsystem that I could not compile offline.

8The desk stays manual

The earlier plan spent a lot of effort on getting the Humanscale desk to obey a computer: opening the handset, emulating its buttons, closing the loop on height, and defending against a button stuck pressed. That was the highest-risk item, because Humanscale publishes no control protocol and I could not verify your handset's electronics.

By keeping the desk at your height for the whole trip, all of that goes away. The desk's own handset and control box stay untouched, and you raise and lower it yourself.

What this removes

  • The optocoupler or relay board, the desk control board and its watchdog.
  • The logic analyzer and spare handset, and the bench-test phase (2 to 4 weeks).
  • The risk of a stuck button driving the desk to a limit, and any warranty question about opening the handset.
  • Waiting for the desk to lower and raise: trips are about 17 s shorter in the sim.

What it adds, and what to do about it

  • A taller, more top-heavy robot while moving. This becomes the main physical risk (section 7, risk 2). Measure the real center of gravity and test at full height before setting speed and braking limits.
  • Loose items on the desk. The sim's braking limits are sized below the slip threshold of typical items, but a monitor and cables are what to test first.
  • The UPS under the desktop. Its weight counts against the lift's rated load, it rides up and down with the desk, and the columns will feel sideways loads they were not built for. See section 7, risk 3.
  • Power. The desk's control box still needs mains power, from the UPS or the dock. Check that the UPS handles the lift motors' start-up current, preferably with a pure sine wave output.
Optional safety aid. A read-only height sensor mounted on the frame (not wired into the Humanscale) lets the software cap speed when the desk is left tall. It is listed in the BOM as optional.

If you want automatic height later

Nothing in the design blocks it. The controller already talks to a desk-lift interface, and the earlier approach still applies: emulate the handset buttons with optocouplers, hold them only while a watchdog is alive, and read the height with a sensor. Do not attempt it until a spare handset has been studied on the bench.

Mechanical note. Confirm the desk's rated load covers the desktop, monitor and anything else it carries. The UPS and batteries ride in the frame, not on the desk.

9Charging station: plug-in, and safe if you forget to unplug

You asked how to set up a charging station for the 12 V FRC battery and the UPS so they can be plugged in, and nothing bad happens if the cord is still plugged in when the desk is summoned. The plan is a simple manual plug-in station, with two independent protections against a forgotten cord. Automatic contacts stay available as a later upgrade.

The station

  1. On the wall: a GFCI outlet on its own 15 A circuit (have an electrician do it) and a hook for the cord. The cord is only long enough (about 0.9 m in the sim) to reach the desk's parking spot.
  2. One cord: a UL-listed, light-duty magnetic breakaway power cord from the wall to the robot.
  3. On the robot: a shrouded AC inlet fixed to the frame, not the UPS, because the UPS now rides up and down with the desktop. From the inlet, a flexible, strain-relieved cable with slack for the full height range goes to the UPS input, which charges its own battery.
  4. The 12 V battery: a small smart charger on the robot, powered from the same inlet (so it only runs when plugged in), connected through a fuse straight to the battery terminals, ahead of the E-stop contactor so that the E-stop never interrupts charging.

If you forget to unplug: two independent layers

  1. Interlock (prevents it, and holds it). Three separate signals say "plugged in": an AC-presence sensor on the inlet, the UPS "on line" status read over USB, and the breakaway connector's auxiliary contact. If any of them says plugged in, the wheels get no command at all, checked on every control loop, not just when you press Come to me. A trip already under way is cancelled and the desk brakes to a stop if you plug in mid-trip (tested in the simulator: it stopped within a few centimeters). It also flashes a beacon and a phone alert saying "unplug me". Three sources because any one can fail silently.
    Hardware backup (recommended): an inlet-empty switch, closed only when the inlet is empty, wired in series with the drive contactor coil, with a key switch for maintenance override. A broken wire then reads as plugged in and the wheels cannot be powered even if the software crashes. Charging is unaffected, because the 12 V charger connects ahead of the contactor.
  2. Breakaway (limits the damage). If it drives anyway (interlock bypassed, a sensor failed, or someone plugged in after the summon started), the connector separates at a low pull force and can be re-mated without tools. Pick a connector that leaves no exposed live pins on the wall side, and route the cord so it cannot reach a wheel.

The release force is the part I cannot verify. Industrial breakaways exist, but the one I checked needs 130 lb to separate, far too stiff here: it would pull the plug out of the wall first. Going home on its own

With a manual cord the robot can still drive itself to the station when a battery is low, but then it waits for a person to plug it in and says so. The thresholds are unchanged: go home below 25% (robot battery) or 20% (UPS) while idle, refuse a new trip below 8%. The UPS reports its charge over USB (check that Network UPS Tools supports your model); the 12 V battery's state comes from its voltage and the PDH's current readings.

What the simulator shows

The simulator has a Dock type selector (Manual cord or Auto contacts), a Plug in / Unplug button, and an Interlock checkbox. Tested: docked but unplugged does not charge and shows "PLUG ME IN"; plugging in charges; Come to me and Learn the room are refused while plugged in; plugging in mid-trip stops the desk within a few centimeters with 0 collisions; with the interlock off the breakaway releases and the trip completes with 0 collisions. In Auto contacts mode there is no cord, so charging does not stop it leaving: the contacts simply disengage.

Wireless charging for the 12 V battery: possible, but I would skip it for v1

You asked whether a wireless charger for the 12 V AGM battery is possible. It is, at this power level. There is a development kit for robots rated up to 100 W; research on automated guided vehicles reports about 80% efficiency charging lead-acid batteries wirelessly, and industrial inductive chargers claim up to 93%. A student project for a small vehicle targeted at least 75% efficiency even with the coils misaligned, and relied on camera-assisted parking to get sub-centimeter alignment.

My rough arithmetic: a 12 V, 17 Ah AGM battery holds about 200 Wh. A 100 W link at 80% efficiency delivers about 80 W, so refilling half the battery takes roughly an hour and a half, plus the slow final phase, and about 20 W is lost as heat.

It would need a transmitter pad on the floor, a receiver under the frame, a rectifier and an AGM-style charge controller on the robot (bulk, absorption and float stages, with temperature compensation), a way to switch the pad on only when the robot is there, and much more precise parking than today's: the simulator parks within about 6 cm of the dock.

Why I would still skip it:

  • It does not remove the cord. The UPS needs 120 V AC. Only the 12 V battery would go wireless, so you would keep the plug and all its protections.
  • A do-it-yourself resonant charger at 100 W is a real power-electronics project. If you try it, buy a kit rather than design one.
  • It costs more and wastes energy compared with a plain charger.

If you later want a fully cordless design, the first thing to check is whether the UPS can be dropped. The desk's control box takes mains power (I have not verified its input), so that needs an answer first. Whatever you try, bench-test with a spare AGM battery before wiring anything to the robot.

Later upgrade

Automatic charging can reuse the same frame inlet with a blind-mate connector and a contactor that energizes it only when docked. The BOM lists that sensor and contactor as optional.

Mains safety. Shrouded connectors, GFCI, fusing, and an electrician's review of the wall station. Do not leave it charging unattended until you have run many supervised cycles.

10Phone app (iOS)

You need an iOS app for this project. Here is what I would build first, how it talks to the robot, and what I have and have not verified.

What version 1 does

  • Shows the robot's status in plain words, both batteries, whether the cord is plugged in, whether it is charging, and the E-stop state (read-only).
  • Shows the room map with the desk, people, and what the robot remembers. You tap where you are.
  • Buttons: Come to me, Return to start, Learn the room, and a software Stop.
  • Alerts such as "unplug me", low battery and stuck, as local notifications.

What it deliberately does not do

It is not a safety device. The Stop button is software only, the app can never release the hardware E-stop, and the cord lock holds the wheels off whatever the app sends. There is no remote steering.

How it connects

iPhone app, to a small bridge on the Pi or Jetson over HTTP and JSON on your local network, to the roboRIO's NetworkTables and the UPS. The phone does not speak NetworkTables itself; the bridge translates. Every call carries an access token, the token is kept in the iOS Keychain, and the bridge is never exposed to the internet. iOS asks permission for local network access, and plain HTTP is allowed to local addresses only. The contract is in API.md in summon_ios.zip.

The one network decision to make early: the phone and the robot must be on the same network. An FRC radio can act as the access point (the phone then has no internet while connected), or the Pi can join your normal Wi-Fi.

Finding you

Version 1 uses a tap on the map. Automatic "find me" is a later step: recent iPhones have a UWB chip that can range to a UWB accessory on the robot through Apple's NearbyInteraction framework, which needs extra hardware and its own project. I have not verified that path.

Alerts when the app is closed

Local notifications only fire while the app is running. To get alerts when it is closed you need Apple push notifications through a small server, or a push relay service. That is a version 2 item.

What I built and tested

  • The contract (API.md): 8 endpoints, status codes, and four rules the real bridge must keep.
  • A bridge that serves the real simulator over that contract, so the app can be developed with no hardware. Tested with curl: the token check, state, map, summon, an off-map target (422), the cord refusal (409 with the reason and the alert flag), Learn refused while plugged in, a summon accepted after unplugging, the soft stop, and error handling.
  • SwiftUI source (7 files, about 550 lines): status card, tap-to-place map, command buttons, settings, polling client, Keychain, local notifications. I could not compile it, because there is no Swift toolchain where I worked. A check confirmed that the braces and brackets balance. Expect a few small compile fixes in Xcode.

What you need

A Mac with Xcode and an iPhone. To distribute through TestFlight you need the Apple Developer Program (about $99 a year; check the current price). As I understand it, a free account can install on your own phone, but those builds expire after 7 days. My estimate is 3 to 5 weeks of part-time work to finish version 1 on top of the bridge, in parallel with the hardware stages.

11Safety plan

  • Layers that do not need software: two hardwired E-stops through a safety relay and contactor; bumpers; a breakaway connector on the charging cord.
  • Layers that do: a charging-cord lock that holds the wheels off, on every loop, while any of three sensors says the cord is plugged in, plus a hardware inlet-empty switch in the contactor coil path; the swept-footprint supervisor (checks the desk's real rectangle against live lidar points and caps speed so it can always stop), a speed cap of 0.8 m/s, current limits, and the IMU tilt fault that stops the robot if it leans too far.
  • Because the desk drives tall: speed and braking limits set from measured tip-over margin at full height, a read-only height sensor to slow down when the desk is raised, and a rule that loose items stay off the desk.
  • Visible and audible: an amber beacon and buzzer while moving.
  • Progression: tethered and unloaded first; then loaded in an empty room; people only after the stopping and tilt tests pass. Always have an E-stop within reach.
  • Paper: a short written risk assessment before people are near it. Mobile-robot standards such as ISO 3691-4 and ANSI/RIA R15.08 are good references; I am not claiming this design meets them.
